DeepFedNAS: Efficient Hardware-Aware
Architecture Adaptation for Heterogeneous IoT
Federations via Pareto-Guided Supernet Training

arXiv:2601.15127v4 [cs.LG] 25 Sep 2026

Bostan Khan and Masoud Daneshtalab

Abstract—Deploying federated learning across heterogeneous
IoT device fleets requires tailored neural network architectures
for each device class, yet existing Federated Neural Architecture
Search (FedNAS) methods suffer from unguided supernet training and prohibitively costly post-training search pipelines that
validate thousands of subnets to construct learned accuracy predictors. We introduce DeepFedNAS, a two-phase framework built
on a multi-objective fitness function that synthesizes informationtheoretic network metrics with architectural heuristics. In the
first phase, Federated Pareto Optimal Supernet Training replaces
random subnet sampling with a pre-computed cache of elite,
high-fitness architectures, yielding a superior supernet. In the
second phase, a Predictor-Free Search uses the structural fitness
function as an accuracy proxy without constructing a learned
subnet-accuracy predictor. In our CIFAR-10 benchmark, preparing the baseline predictor requires evaluating 10,000 subnets over
the 5,000-image validation split, totaling 50 million image-level
forward evaluations. DeepFedNAS eliminates these evaluations
and selects a hardware-optimized architecture in ∼20 seconds on
a CPU. Experiments on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and CINIC-10
demonstrate state-of-the-art accuracy and robust performance
under extreme non-IID conditions (α = 0.1). On CIFAR100, DeepFedNAS provides an average 2.12-percentage-point
gain across the four computation-budget intervals. Under the
lowest evaluated computation budget, its mean result exceeds
SuperFedNAS’s best mean accuracy while using 2.95× fewer
parameters. These results make DeepFedNAS practical for scalable, communication-constrained IoT federations. Source code:
https://github.com/bostankhan6/DeepFedNAS

created networks of billions of devices with profoundly heterogeneous hardware profiles, spanning resource-rich edge
gateways and smart cameras, mid-range wearable health monitors, and severely constrained sensor nodes [2]. Deploying
intelligent inference models across such diverse device fleets
requires architectures that are individually tailored to each

Learning (FL) [3] has emerged as the dominant paradigm
for training these models collaboratively across decentralized
devices without exposing private data, enabling applications
ranging from mobile keyboard prediction [4] to personalized
healthcare analytics [5]. While FL provides a robust solution
for how to train a given model, the challenge of determining what model architecture to deploy on each device class
remains a critical operational bottleneck. This architectural
design, often performed manually, is frequently suboptimal
given the vast heterogeneity of data distributions and hardware
capabilities across IoT devices [6].
In communication-constrained IoT federations, this challenge is compounded by two practical realities. First, every
model parameter transmitted during FL training directly impacts bandwidth consumption and energy expenditure which is
a significant concern for devices communicating over metered
cellular connections (LTE-M, NB-IoT) or low-power wireless
links [7]. Deploying unnecessarily large models wastes this
scarce bandwidth. Second, IoT device fleets are not static:
new hardware platforms, firmware revisions, and deployment
contexts emerge continuously, requiring deployment pipelines
that can rapidly produce optimized architectures for new
device classes without costly retraining [8]. These operational
demands motivate the need for automated architecture search
methods that are both communication-efficient and rapidly
adaptable.
To automate this critical design step, the research community has developed Federated Neural Architecture Search
(FedNAS) to discover optimal network architectures directly
within the federated setting [9]. Among the various FedNAS
techniques, supernet-based methods have become the stateof-the-art due to their high cost efficiency, building on the
success of centralized approaches like the Once-For-All (OFA)
network [10]. The SuperFedNAS framework [11], in particular, marked a key advancement by decoupling the costly
supernet training phase from a fast, training-free search phase.
This innovation effectively reduced the training complexity of
finding specialized subnets for multiple hardware targets from
O(N ) to O(1).
Despite this progress, the SuperFedNAS framework presents
two fundamental challenges that limit its ultimate performance
and practicality for IoT deployments. First, the training of
the supernet itself is unguided and inefficient. The baseline
framework predominantly relies on random sampling of subnets (e.g., the Sandwich Rule’s uniform sampling component

[12]) to update the shared weights. This unguided approach
frequently selects suboptimal architectures, whose subsequent
training provides noisy and conflicting gradient updates that
can degrade the supernet’s shared weights and hinder the
performance of higher-performing subnets, ultimately wasting
scarce communication bandwidth between IoT devices and the
aggregation server. Second, the post-training search for optimal subnets remains a computationally expensive, multi-stage
pipeline. The standard methodology generates a large dataset
of architecture–accuracy pairs through subnet evaluation and
fits a separate accuracy predictor before search. In our CIFAR10 benchmark, constructing this predictor requires 10,000
complete validation-set subnet evaluations, corresponding to
50 million image-level forward evaluations. This creates a
substantial GPU-dependent post-training bottleneck.
In this paper, we introduce DeepFedNAS (illustrated in
Fig. 1), a novel, two-phase framework that comprehensively
addresses these limitations to significantly advance federated supernet training and search. Our work is grounded
in the observation that a mathematically motivated approach
to architecture evaluation can fundamentally transform both
the supernet training process and the subsequent search for
optimal subnets. Inspired by the mathematical design concepts
of DeepMAD [13], we construct a unified multi-objective
fitness function, F(A), that synthesizes network information
theory with empirical architectural heuristics. Crucially, we
operationalize structural guidelines (like depth uniformity and
channel monotonicity) as penalty terms, thereby enabling a
holistic, single-objective optimization for all desired architectural properties within a federated, weight-sharing context.
This unified fitness function underpins our two primary contributions.
First, to address the supernet training inefficiency, we propose Federated Pareto Optimal Supernet Training. Leveraging
our fitness function, we perform an extensive, independent
search to generate a pre-computed cache of Pareto-optimal
architectures, which we term the “Pareto path”. This path
serves as a Pareto-Guided training curriculum. Instead of
random sampling, our methodology ensures the supernet’s
shared weights are consistently updated by gradients from
theoretically grounded, high-fitness architectures, thereby producing a superior final supernet. Additionally, to fully enable
this guided search and overcome inherent limitations of prior
supernet implementations, we introduce a redesigned, generic
ResNet-based [14] supernet framework that significantly expands the architectural search space and improves model
fitness.
Second, we leverage the properties of our optimal pathguided trained supernet to introduce a Predictor-Free Search

II. R ELATED W ORK
A. Federated Learning
FL enables distributed learning without centralizing private data [15]. FedAvg [3] established the standard clienttraining/server-aggregation paradigm [16]–[18]. Our work centers on statistical heterogeneity, where non-IID data degrades
performance [19], [20], and system heterogeneity, where client
resources differ [6], motivating models adapted to both local
distributions [21], [22] and hardware budgets [23].

3

B. Federated Learning for IoT and Edge Intelligence
FL is attractive for distributed IoT intelligence, but IoT
deployments intensify resource and communication heterogeneity [2], [24]. Devices range from GPU-equipped cameras
to microcontroller-class sensors [8], while bandwidth-limited
links make transmitted model size a practical bottleneck [7].
These conditions favor methods that train once yet expose
architectures for different device classes. Prior work has used
knowledge distillation [25] and pruning [26]; DeepFedNAS
instead targets efficient supernet training and rapid hardwareaware specialization.
C. Neural Architecture Search with Weight Sharing
NAS automates architecture design. Early reinforcementlearning [27] and evolutionary [28] methods were costly,
while weight-sharing supernets [29], [30] and DARTS [31]
improved efficiency. OFA [10] supports multiple hardware
constraints from one supernet, while BigNAS [32] popularized
the “Sandwich Rule” [12]. Shared weights can nevertheless
yield poor rank correlation with standalone accuracy [33],
partly due to multi-model forgetting [34], [35], motivating
improved subnet sampling [36], [37].
D. Federated Neural Architecture Search
FedNAS combines FL and NAS under decentralized data
and hardware constraints [9], [38], [39]. Existing work targets either data personalization or system efficiency. Datapersonalized methods adapt subnet paths or structures to
local distributions [40]–[46]. System-oriented methods expose
differently sized subnets for heterogeneous clients [26], but
tightly coupled search and training can remain costly [47],
[48], and restricted search spaces can limit deployment diversity [39].
SuperFedNAS [11] addresses search cost by decoupling
supernet training from post-training architecture search. Nevertheless, it still relies on unguided random subnet sampling and
a learned accuracy predictor for post-training search. DeepFedNAS targets both stages: fitness-guided sampling improves
supernet training, while the same structural fitness function
supports Predictor-Free Search. This direction is related to
centralized DC-NAS [36], which diversifies sampling, and
PreNAS [37], which uses a zero-cost proxy to emphasize highquality candidates.
E. Mathematical Frameworks for Architecture Design
Most NAS methods optimize architectures through learned
or empirical search signals, including gradients [31], evolution [49], and more recently LLM-derived priors [50], [51].
DeepMAD [13] instead uses intrinsic mathematical metrics
related to network information entropy and effectiveness,
while other approaches use mutual-information analysis [52].
DeepFedNAS adapts this theory-driven perspective to federated supernet training by combining mathematical metrics with
structural heuristics in a unified fitness function used for both
guided training and Predictor-Free Search.

III. M ETHODOLOGY
DeepFedNAS is organized into two main phases, implemented through three connected operational stages. Phase 1,
Federated Pareto Optimal Supernet Training, consists of an
offline architecture search that constructs a cache of highfitness architectures (Section III-D), followed by federated
supernet training using this cache as a subnet-assignment
curriculum (Section III-E). Phase 2, Predictor-Free Search,
ranks candidate deployment architectures using the same structural fitness function (Section III-F). The architecture-only
structural fitness function underlying both phases is defined in
Section III-C. We also use a redesigned generic ResNet-based
supernet (Section III-B) to support the search space considered
in this work. Figure 1 summarizes these stages. We begin by
defining the federated supernet training problem.

F. Entropy-Based Fitness as a Predictor for Efficient Search
A major contribution of DeepFedNAS is a Predictor-Free
Search Method that circumvents the high computational cost
of constructing learned accuracy predictors. This capability
stems directly from our unified multi-objective fitness formulation, F(A), and our Path-Guided Training strategy.
We posit that by constraining the supernet training to a
curriculum of high-fitness architectures (via the Pareto Path
Subnet Cache), we condition the shared weights W ∗ such that
the structural fitness F(A) becomes a useful structural proxy

G. Additional Hardware Constraints for Deployment
Our framework supports hardware-aware deployment by
integrating constraints such as parameters and latency into the
final search.
1) Integrating Model Parameters: For memory-limited devices, we integrate an explicit upper bound on parameters
(Params(A)) as a hard constraint during the deployment
search:
Params(A) ≤ ParamBudgetM
(13)
2) Integrating Inference Latency: We employ a Latency
Predictor Model (LPM) to estimate inference latency, denoted
as Lpred (A, Device). It is crucial to distinguish the computational nature of this LPM from the accuracy predictors
used in prior work. Accuracy predictors require the generation
of a dataset containing (subnet architecture, accuracy) pairs.
Constructing this dataset is prohibitively expensive because
measuring the ground-truth accuracy of a single subnet necessitates a full inference pass over the entire validation set.
Iterating this process for thousands of architectures to train a
predictor creates a computational bottleneck.
In contrast, training an LPM requires a dataset of (subnet
architecture, latency) pairs. For each sampled architecture, one
input sample (e.g., one image tensor) is profiled on the target
device over a small number of repeated timed runs, and the
measured inference times are averaged to reduce timing noise.
This single-sample-per-architecture profiling protocol is independent of the validation dataset size and is substantially less
costly than collecting an accuracy label, which requires a full
validation-set evaluation. Thus, DeepFedNAS eliminates the
costly accuracy-predictor pipeline while using a lightweight
latency predictor only when latency is the deployment constraint.

IV. E XPERIMENTS
We conduct a series of experiments to validate the effectiveness of the DeepFedNAS framework. Our evaluation is
designed to answer four primary research questions:
1) Does Pareto Optimal Supernet Training produce
a superior supernet? We compare subnets extracted
from the DeepFedNAS-trained supernet against the SuperFedNAS baseline, which trains the same redesigned
expanded supernet using random subnet sampling.
2) How effective is our Predictor-Free Search? We
evaluate the quality of architectures found by our ondemand fitness-driven search and quantify the reduction
in computational cost compared to traditional accuracypredictor pipelines.
3) How robust is our methodology? We analyze the
performance of our framework under varying FL conditions, including data heterogeneity and sparse client
participation.
4) How does DeepFedNAS support hardware-aware
deployment constraints? We characterize the parameter
and device-specific latency trade-offs of selected subnets
and show how these quantities can be incorporated into
the deployment search.
We begin by detailing our experimental setup before presenting our main results and ablation studies.

A. Experimental Setup
a) Datasets and Data Partitioning: Our experiments
use CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, and CINIC-10. For each CIFAR dataset, we create a fixed stratified 45,000/5,000 training/validation split from the official training set and partition
only the 45,000 training images among clients. For CINIC-10,
we use its official training, validation, and test directories for
the corresponding roles. Statistical heterogeneity is introduced
by partitioning training data among clients using a Dirichlet
distribution [53]. The main comparison uses α = 100; the
heterogeneity analysis uses α ∈ {100, 1, 0.1} (Section IV-C).
We use K = 20 clients for CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 and
K = 100 clients for CINIC-10.
b) Evaluation Protocol: The validation split is used
for checkpoint selection and for any data-dependent search
decisions, while the DeepFedNAS deployment search itself
ranks candidates using the architecture-based fitness F(A)
without validation-set evaluation. The best checkpoint maximizes the mean validation accuracy of the four reference
subnets evaluated every 20 rounds. For the SuperFedNAS
baseline, validation data are also the only data used to construct and fit its accuracy predictor. We perform five posttraining architecture searches with seeds 42–46, record the
selected architectures, and then evaluate them once on the
untouched official test set. The searches use a population
of 256, 512 generations, and mutation probability 0.3. Thus,
no test example is used for federated training, checkpoint
selection, SuperFedNAS predictor construction, architecture
search, correlation analysis, or architecture selection. Reported
± values summarize variation across the five searches. A
value of 0.00 means that all five searches selected the same
executable architecture, which commonly occurs at the allmaximum endpoint.
c) Supernetwork Architecture: To evaluate our method
across a complex design space, we utilize the generic ResNetstyle supernet framework detailed in Section III-B. For these
experiments, we instantiate a 4-stage supernet with base
channel sizes of [256, 512, 1024, 2048]. The search space
is defined by three variable dimensions: depth di ∈ {0, 1, 2}
additional blocks per stage, corresponding to 1+di ∈ {1, 2, 3}
total blocks per stage; width w with multipliers from 0.1
to 1.0 in steps of 0.1; and bottleneck expansion ratios e ∈
{0.1, 0.14, 0.18, 0.22, 0.25}. This results in a combinatorial
space of approximately 1.98 × 1015 architectures, significantly
larger than prior work (see Table I).

across heterogeneous IoT device fleets. We analyze three key
dimensions: communication overhead, fleet-wide architecture
specialization, and scalability to new device classes.
a) Communication Overhead Reduction: In federated
IoT systems, the volume of model parameters transmitted
per round affects bandwidth consumption and communication
energy. As shown in Fig. 3, the controlled CIFAR-100 comparison finds that DeepFedNAS uses fewer mean parameters
in the first three matched MAC intervals while attaining
higher accuracy in every interval. This provides a useful
model-payload proxy for deployments that select subnets in
these intervals. Actual network traffic additionally depends
on participation, serialization, protocol overhead, and any
compression, and is therefore not estimated from parameter
counts alone.
b) Fleet-Wide Architecture Specialization: A defining
characteristic of IoT federations is the coexistence of devices
with vastly different resource budgets. Consider a representative smart-home deployment comprising: (i) a security
camera with an edge GPU (∼2–3B MACs budget), (ii) a
smart thermostat with a mid-range processor (∼1B MACs),
and (iii) a lightweight sensor hub (∼0.5B MACs). After a
single supernet training phase, DeepFedNAS can specialize
an architecture for each device class through independent CPU
searches of ∼20 seconds each. The SuperFedNAS baseline, by
contrast, first requires construction of its 10,000-architecture
accuracy predictor, which takes approximately 3.9 hours on an
NVIDIA RTX A5000. DeepFedNAS instead uses a one-time,
approximately 20-minute CPU Pareto-cache construction step
before supernet training, allowing a single trained supernet
to serve diverse deployment budgets without post-training
accuracy-label collection or accuracy-predictor fitting.
c) Scalability to New Device Classes: IoT device ecosystems are not static; new hardware platforms, firmware revisions, and deployment contexts emerge continuously. A critical
advantage of the DeepFedNAS pipeline is that accommodating a new device class requires only a lightweight search
against the pre-trained supernet. No retraining, new accuracylabel collection, or accuracy-predictor fitting is needed. If a
latency constraint is desired, only lightweight single-sampleper-architecture profiling (Section III-G2) is required to collect
labels for the latency predictor, a process that is substantially
cheaper than the full accuracy evaluation pipeline. This “plugand-search” capability is essential for IoT operators managing
evolving, heterogeneous fleets at scale.

select N = 60 for all main experiments. This value provides a
safety margin that ensures a dense representation of the Pareto
training curriculum, while remaining computationally efficient.
2) Component-wise Ablation of Fitness Function: To validate the individual contributions of the DeepMAD-inspired
fitness components defined in Eq. 5, we conducted an ablation study at a constrained computational budget of ∼600M
MACs on CIFAR-10. We performed independent evolutionary
searches (N = 5) for each configuration and evaluated the
mean test accuracy of the discovered subnets. The results are
summarized in Table VI.
a) Impact of Effectiveness (ρ): The DeepMAD baseline
yields an accuracy of 92.60%. Introducing the effectiveness
reward (ρ) improves performance by 0.78%, suggesting that
favoring balanced depth-to-width ratios helps identify architectures that perform well in practice.
b) Impact of Structural Alignment: Incorporating structural regularizers yields further gains. The Depth Variance
Penalty (Q), which encourages balanced stage depths, improves accuracy to 93.41%. Finally, the Non-Decreasing Channel Penalty (V ), which enforces standard channel expansion
conventions, boosts the final accuracy to 93.62% (+1.02%

V. L IMITATIONS AND F UTURE W ORK
Our evaluation is limited to ResNet-style convolutional
search spaces and image-classification benchmarks (CIFAR10, CIFAR-100, CINIC-10). Extending Pareto-Guided Supernet Training to Transformers, IoT-specific tasks, and physical
hardware platforms such as NVIDIA Jetson, Raspberry Pi,
and ARM Cortex-M will require architecture-family-specific
search spaces and fitness definitions followed by task-level validation. Quantization, mixed precision, and joint architecture–
precision search are also left for future work; such an extension
would add precision choices to the architecture genome and
require a device-specific resource model.

13

Consistent with SuperFedNAS [11], we assume clients can
train their assigned subnets. DeepFedNAS therefore optimizes
deployment architectures but does not enforce hard client
hardware constraints during training. A natural extension is
system-aware sampling that restricts client-subnet assignments
using device capabilities such as available RAM and processor
type.
Finally, Predictor-Free Search depends on the correlation
between structural fitness F(A) and accuracy. For the 60
CIFAR-10 architectures in Fig. 6, the Spearman correlation is
ρ = 0.95. Under substantial domain shift or radically different
modalities, learned predictors or task-specific calibration of the
structural regularizers may provide more reliable ranking.
VI. C ONCLUSION
DeepFedNAS addresses two bottlenecks in federated architecture adaptation for heterogeneous IoT fleets: unguided
supernet training and costly post-training accuracy prediction.
Its unified fitness function enables Federated Pareto Optimal
Supernet Training, which trains from an elite architecture curriculum, and Predictor-Free Search, which specializes subnets
for target resource budgets in ∼20 seconds. In our CIFAR10 benchmark, this removes the baseline workload of 10,000
subnet evaluations over 5,000 validation images, or 50 million
image-level forward evaluations. Across three datasets and
diverse non-IID settings, DeepFedNAS achieves state-of-theart accuracy. On CIFAR-100, its lowest-budget result exceeds
SuperFedNAS’s best mean accuracy while using 2.95× fewer
parameters. These capabilities support rapid, resource-aware
deployment without supernet retraining or learned accuracypredictor construction.
